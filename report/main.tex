\author{Yutong Han}
\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}
\noindent We backtest three equity strategies built from option-market and earnings information on 15 large US stocks, using 2022--2023 to fit and 2024 as an out-of-sample year. Trades fill at official auction prices and pay a realistic fee schedule. Option-flow stock selection, which buys stocks with net option buying and sells stocks with net option selling, earned 8.6\% a year over cash in 2022--2023 and 12.9\% in 2024 (Sharpe ratios 0.96 and 1.71). Earnings-announcement drift, which trades in the direction of the market's reaction to an earnings release, earned 3.4\% and 12.8\%. A pre-earnings premium strategy earned 2.4\% in 2022--2023 and 0.4\% in 2024. An equal-weight portfolio of the three, with idle capital earning SOFR, returned 14.91\% in 2024 against 5.40\% for SOFR alone. None of these results is statistically strong: the fitting-period $t$-statistics are below 2, and the three strategies were chosen from a longer list partly on their 2024 results. We therefore propose paper trading the portfolio on 2025 data before committing capital.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

This report covers three strategies and one way to combine them. All three trade stocks, and two of them use information from the option market.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Option-flow stock selection} (Section~\ref{sec:flow}). Traders with private information often prefer options. When option buying in a stock outweighs option selling, the stock tends to rise the next day. We buy the stocks with the most net option buying and short those with the most net selling.
  \item \textbf{Earnings-announcement drift} (Section~\ref{sec:drift}). Prices tend to under-react to earnings news. We trade in the direction of the stock's reaction to an announcement and hold for 20 trading days.
  \item \textbf{Pre-earnings premium} (Section~\ref{sec:pre}). Stocks have tended to earn more in the days just before scheduled earnings. We hold stocks for the five sessions before an announcement and sell before the news.
\end{itemize}

Section~\ref{sec:portfolio} combines the three. Each strategy uses only part of its capital at a time, so the portfolio places all unused capital in SOFR, the overnight secured funding rate published by the New York Fed. Section~\ref{sec:limits} sets out what the evidence can and cannot support.

These three strategies are what remained from a longer search. We tested 25 strategy specifications on the same data, including several option-premium strategies, and Appendix~\ref{app:other} summarizes the ones we dropped. Appendix~\ref{app:impl} describes the implementation.

\section{Data, trading rules and costs}\label{sec:data}

\paragraph{Data.} The sample runs from 2022-01-03 to 2024-12-31 (753 trading days). The universe is 15 large US stocks with listed options: AAPL, AMD, AMZN, AVGO, CRM, GOOGL, JPM, LLY, MSFT, NFLX, NVDA, TSLA, UNH, WMT and XOM. We use four sources:
\begin{itemize}
  \item intraday option trades, each with the prevailing best bid and offer;
  \item end-of-day option chains, with implied volatility, delta and open interest;
  \item equity prints from the opening and closing auctions;
  \item daily stock data, including split and dividend adjustments, and earnings announcement dates and times.
\end{itemize}
SPY, the S\&P 500 ETF, is used for hedging. SOFR fixings come from the New York Fed.

\paragraph{How trades fill.} All three strategies trade stocks only, at two kinds of price.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Closing trades} fill at the official closing price, which equals the closing-auction print on every day we checked.
  \item \textbf{Opening trades} fill at the primary exchange's opening-auction price. If that print is missing, the order fills at the same day's close.
\end{itemize}
Auction trades do not cross a bid--ask spread, so the main costs are fees and borrowing. A strategy starts with a \$1M account. Idle cash earns the 1-month Treasury bill rate inside each strategy; the portfolio in Section~\ref{sec:portfolio} uses SOFR instead.

\paragraph{How we judge the results.} We fix each strategy's rules using 2022--2023, the \emph{fitting period}, and evaluate them once on 2024, the \emph{out-of-sample year}. The 2024 run used code that was committed before 2024 data entered the simulation. We report annualized return in excess of cash, the Sharpe ratio, and a Newey--West $t$-statistic \citep{neweywest1987}. For event strategies we also report the average return per event. Three years of data is short: an annualized Sharpe ratio estimated over one year has a standard error of about 1, so differences between strategies of that size are not meaningful.

\section{Limitations and next steps}\label{sec:limits}

\paragraph{Statistical strength.} No result here is strong enough to rely on. The fitting-period $t$-statistics are 1.38 for option flow, 0.87 for earnings drift and 1.08 for the pre-earnings premium. The deflated Sharpe ratio \citep{bailey2014} adjusts for the number of variants tried, 88 across our last two rounds of testing. It is 0.13, 0.05 and 0.09 for the three strategies. A value near 1 would indicate a result unlikely to come from searching alone.

\paragraph{Selection.} We kept these three strategies after seeing how all 25 performed, including in 2024. Each one's 2024 result is a genuine out-of-sample test of rules fixed in advance, but the choice of which three to present is not.

\paragraph{Data.} The universe has 15 stocks, of which about 12 are tradeable on a typical day because some lack auction data. The option trade record is a sample of all trades rather than the full tape, so the flow signal is measured with noise. Fees follow a public schedule rather than executed broker statements.

\paragraph{Next steps.} The rules for all three strategies and the portfolio are frozen in code. The natural next test is a single run on 2025 data with no changes. The option-flow signal would also benefit from a larger universe: its strength comes from ranking many stocks each day, and 15 names limits that.

\section{Implementation}\label{app:impl}

\paragraph{Code.} Each strategy is a C++ program that reads reduced data files, applies the rules day by day, and writes a trade list and a daily return series (\texttt{engines/option\_flow.cpp}, \texttt{engines/earnings\_events.cpp}). Python scripts reduce the raw data with DuckDB and compute the statistics (\texttt{analysis/scorecard.py}, \texttt{analysis/portfolio.py}). The notebook \texttt{notebooks/figures.ipynb} draws the figures.

\paragraph{Accounting.} Stock positions are marked to the official close every day. Splits adjust share counts, and dividends are credited on the ex-date. Each strategy refuses to simulate dates after a configured end date, which is how the 2024 data were held back during development.

\paragraph{Checks.} We reconciled the stock-position ledger by hand: price gains of \$111,375, less \$861 in fees, plus \$797 in dividends, match its \$111,311 total exactly. Rebuilding the engines from a clean copy of the repository reproduces the stored results byte for byte.
